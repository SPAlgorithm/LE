# Video 2: Build any number from prime quadruplets

Target: ~11 minutes, ~1,700 words of narration, 25 scenes.
Tone: excited, playful, hands-on.

---

## Scene 1 — Cold open
VISUAL: hero:16 (eight tiles on a table)
NARRATION: Give me any whole number. Fifty three. Seventy six. Nine hundred ninety nine million. I can build it from prime quadruplets, using nothing but plus, minus and times. Every single number, no exceptions, and I can prove it. This is the second half of the Ladhe quad story, and it is the part you can play with at the kitchen table.

## Scene 2 — The pieces
VISUAL: hero:2 (royal quad on a throne)
NARRATION: Here are the pieces. The royal quad: two, three, five, seven. The first prime quadruplet: eleven, thirteen, seventeen, nineteen, four primes packed as tightly as four primes can be. Later, more quadruplets join: one hundred one to one hundred nine, one ninety one to one ninety nine, and so on. Each piece may be used once.

## Scene 3 — The rules
VISUAL: rulecard:Quadruplet members are only ever added|Royal members 2, 3, 5, 7 may be added, subtracted or multiplied, each used once|Only quadruplet members smaller than the target may be used
NARRATION: Three rules. Quadruplet members are only ever added, never subtracted. The royal members may be added, subtracted or multiplied, in any expression you like, but each royal member appears at most once. And to build a number, you may only use quadruplet members smaller than it. That's it.

## Scene 4 — Warm up: small numbers
VISUAL: equations:1 = 3 - 2|4 = 7 - 3|6 = 5 + 3 - 2|10 = 7 + 3
NARRATION: Warm up with the royal quad alone. One is three minus two. Four is seven minus three. Six is five plus three minus two. Ten is seven plus three. Keep going and you will find that two, three, five and seven, with plus, minus and times, reach every number from zero to thirty eight without a single gap.

## Scene 5 — The royal reach
VISUAL: equations:23 = (3*7) + 2|33 = (5*7) - 2|38 = (5*7) + 3
NARRATION: The bigger royal numbers need multiplication. Twenty three is three times seven plus two. Thirty three is five times seven minus two. Thirty eight is five times seven plus three. Thirty eight is where the royal quad runs out of steam: every number up to it, and then the first hole.

## Scene 6 — The first hole
VISUAL: equations:39 = 19 + 17 + 3
NARRATION: Thirty nine is the first number the royal quad cannot make. Try it, you will not find a way. But now bring in the quadruplet. Nineteen plus seventeen plus three. Done. The quadruplet members plug the holes the royal members leave, and the royal members plug the gaps between the quadruplet members. They need each other.

## Scene 7 — Sixteen
VISUAL: equations:16 = 11 + 5|16 = 13 + 3|16 = (3*7) - 5
NARRATION: Sixteen. Eleven plus five. Or thirteen plus three. Or three times seven minus five, using royal members only. Many roads lead to the same number, and that freedom is exactly what makes the whole thing work.

## Scene 8 — Fifty three
VISUAL: equations:53 = 19 + 11 + (7*5) - 2|53 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 7 - 3
NARRATION: Fifty three. One way: nineteen plus eleven, that is thirty, then seven times five is thirty five, minus two. Thirty plus thirty five minus two is fifty three. Another way, the one the tool picks: take the biggest members first. Nineteen, seventeen, thirteen, that is forty nine. Four to go. Seven minus three. Fifty three.

## Scene 9 — Fifty five and the greedy habit
VISUAL: equations:55 = 19 + 11 + (7*5)|55 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 5 + 3 - 2
NARRATION: Fifty five. Nineteen plus eleven plus seven times five. Or greedily: nineteen, seventeen, thirteen, then six to go, and six is five plus three minus two. The greedy habit, largest member first, always works, and when the leftover is awkward the tool simply backs up one step and tries a different member.

## Scene 10 — Seventy six and a picky rule
VISUAL: equations:76 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 11 + (3*7) - 5
NARRATION: Seventy six. Nineteen, seventeen, thirteen, eleven, that is sixty. Sixteen to go. Now sixteen has a nested form, two times the sum of five and three, but the tool refuses it. It wants a flat expression you can check in your head: three times seven minus five. If the leftover only has nested forms, it swaps a quadruplet member and tries again.

## Scene 11 — The promise
VISUAL: hero:18 (wall of tiles lighting up)
NARRATION: Here is the promise. With just these eight numbers, two, three, five, seven, eleven, thirteen, seventeen, nineteen, you can build every integer from zero to one hundred one. In fact up to one hundred fifty six. Every one. No exceptions. We checked them all, and then we proved it.

## Scene 12 — The ladder lemma
VISUAL: ladder:38|49|62|79|98|156
NARRATION: The proof is a ladder. Suppose you can already build every number up to some top rung, T. Now add a new piece that is no bigger than T plus one. Every number up to T you already had. Every number from T plus one up to T plus the new piece is the new piece plus something you already had. So the ladder grows by exactly the size of the new piece. Royal quad: thirty eight. Add eleven: forty nine. Thirteen: sixty two. Seventeen: seventy nine. Nineteen: ninety eight. And a few clever royal combinations push it to one hundred fifty six.

## Scene 13 — The ladder never breaks
VISUAL: hero:19 (rocket up the ladder)
NARRATION: Each new quadruplet adds four rungs. After the second quadruplet you reach five hundred seventy six. After the third, one thousand three hundred fifty six. After the fourth, four thousand six hundred fifty six. The ladder always grows faster than the next quadruplet arrives, so it never breaks. Below one billion we checked every step, and beyond that it would take a quadruplet more than four times the sum of all earlier ones to stop it. That has never come close to happening.

## Scene 14 — Crossing into a new quadruplet
VISUAL: equations:100 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 11 + (5*7) + 3 + 2|101 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 11 + (5*7) + (2*3)|102 = 101 + 3 - 2|103 = 101 + 2
NARRATION: Watch what happens at a quadruplet boundary. One hundred still uses the old pieces: nineteen, seventeen, thirteen, eleven, five times seven, three, two. But the moment we pass one hundred one, one hundred one itself becomes a piece. One hundred two is one hundred one plus three minus two. One hundred three is one hundred one plus two. The pool grows with you.

## Scene 15 — A stretch of numbers
VISUAL: equations:60 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 11|61 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 11 + 3 - 2|62 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 11 + 2|63 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 11 + 3|64 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 11 + 7 - 3|65 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 11 + 5
NARRATION: Look at a stretch. Sixty is all four quadruplet members. Sixty one adds three minus two. Sixty two adds two. Sixty three adds three. Sixty four adds seven minus three. Sixty five adds five. The quadruplet members carry the bulk, the royal members fine-tune the last few units. That division of labor is the whole design.

## Scene 16 — Big numbers
VISUAL: equations:999986171 = 999900529 + 82729 + 2089 + 823 + 3 - 2
NARRATION: Does it scale? Here is the last quadruplet below one billion. Nine hundred ninety nine million nine hundred eighty six thousand one hundred seventy one. It is nine hundred ninety nine million nine hundred thousand five hundred twenty nine, plus eighty two thousand seven hundred twenty nine, plus two thousand eighty nine, plus eight hundred twenty three, plus three minus two. Six pieces. For a nine-digit number.

## Scene 17 — Why big numbers are easy
VISUAL: stats:below 999,986,171|113,544 pieces|largest piece taken|999,900,529
NARRATION: Big numbers are actually the easy case. Below a billion there are more than one hundred thirteen thousand pieces to choose from. The largest piece that fits eats almost all of the number, the next largest eats almost all of what is left, and after four or five bites only a royal-sized crumb remains. The hard cases are the small numbers, where the pool is tiny, and those we simply checked one by one.

## Scene 18 — The tool
VISUAL: terminal:python3 lc.py -d 76|76 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 11 + (3*7) - 5|python3 lc.py -d 0|0 = 5 - (3 + 2)
NARRATION: You do not have to do this by hand. The LC tool has a derive switch. Type dash d and a number, and it prints the derivation. Seventy six is nineteen plus seventeen plus thirteen plus eleven plus three times seven minus five. Even zero works: five minus the sum of three and two.

## Scene 19 — Ranges
VISUAL: terminal:python3 lc.py -d 53 83|53 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 7 - 3|54 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 5|55 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 5 + 3 - 2|...|83 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 11 + (3*7) + 2
NARRATION: Give it two numbers and it prints a whole range, one line per integer. Fifty three to eighty three, thirty one derivations, in a blink. It always takes the largest piece first, and it always closes with the simplest royal expression it can find: fewest multiplications, then fewest minus signs, and never a product wrapped around a sum.

## Scene 20 — Why keep it simple
VISUAL: equations:(3*7) - 5 = 16   ✓|(2*(5 + 3)) = 16   ✗
NARRATION: Why so picky about the closing expression? Because a derivation should be readable at a glance. Three times seven minus five you can check in your head. Two times, open bracket, five plus three, close bracket, you have to stop and think. The tool re-chooses its pieces rather than show you a nested expression.

## Scene 21 — What this means
VISUAL: hero:15 (constellation chain)
NARRATION: Step back and look at what we have. The prime quadruplets, plus the four royal primes, form a counting system for all of arithmetic. Every integer has an address written in quadruplet members. That is a strange and wonderful thing for a set of numbers that mathematicians usually describe as scattered at random.

## Scene 22 — Applications, honestly
VISUAL: hero:11 (icons: lock, chain, ledger, classroom)
NARRATION: What is it good for? Honestly, we are at the beginning. Writing numbers as sums of primes is already the basis of a signature scheme. A representation that anyone can verify by simple addition sounds like something a proof or a ledger could use. And it is a wonderful classroom game: hand a student the eight numbers and a target, and watch them find a way.

## Scene 23 — Try it
VISUAL: equations:77 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 11 + 7 + 5 + 3 + 2|88 = ?
NARRATION: Try one right now. Seventy seven. Pause the video. Nineteen, seventeen, thirteen, eleven is sixty. Seventeen to go. Seven plus five plus three plus two. There it is. Now try eighty eight, and notice that you need a royal multiplication for the first time.

## Scene 24 — The answer
VISUAL: equations:88 = 19 + 17 + 13 + 11 + (3*7) + 5 + 2
NARRATION: Here is one answer for eighty eight. Sixty from the quadruplet, then twenty eight to go, and twenty eight is three times seven plus five plus two. If you found a different one, good. Different is allowed. Different is the point.
